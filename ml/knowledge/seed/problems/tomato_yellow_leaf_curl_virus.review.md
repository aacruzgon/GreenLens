## Treatments

- **control_weeds_and_volunteers** (cultural): Pull weeds in and around the garden, and pull up volunteer tomato plants (ones that come up on their own); for late blight, pull volunteer potato plants too. Volunteer tomatoes and weeds can carry bacterial speck and bacterial spot. Tomato-family weeds carry these diseases: black nightshade (early blight, late blight, bacterial spot), Carolina horsenettle (late blight) and ground cherry (bacterial spot). Septoria leaf spot also survives the winter on some weeds related to tomato. Weeds also host whiteflies, which can bring tomato yellow leaf curl virus into the garden from nightshades and jimsonweed. Destroying weeds such as pokeweed, jimsonweed, wild blackberry and wild geranium cuts the number of spider mites that survive the winter.

- **soap_or_oil_spray** (chemical): If you need a spray, try insecticidal soap or canola oil labelled for tomatoes first. Of the sprays, they are the least toxic to people, other non-target organisms and the environment, and nothing stays active once they dry. But they kill any mite or insect they hit, helpful ones included, such as predatory mites. They work only where they touch, so cover the plant thoroughly, especially the undersides of the leaves, where spider mites and whiteflies live and sprays reach them poorly. For whiteflies, spray early, before they build up to large numbers, and aim at the young whiteflies; whiteflies resist many insecticides. Soap and oil can burn leaves: some varieties are more sensitive, and weather, fertilisers and additives can raise the risk. Use a product sold for plants: household soaps, detergents and kitchen oils work less reliably. Follow the label.
